## 1. Democritus and Leucippus: atomos

The ancient Greek philosopher who coined the term "atomos" was Democritus, along with his mentor Leucippus, around 400 BCE.

The word "atomos" literally translates to "uncuttable" or "indivisible" in Greek. Democritus reasoned that if you kept cutting an object in half, you would eventually reach a tiny, fundamental particle that could no longer be divided.

## 2. Aristotle: continuous matter and the four (and five) elements

Aristotle completely rejected the concept of atoms. He strongly opposed Democritus's "atomos" theory and instead argued that matter is continuous and infinitely divisible.

If you cut a piece of matter in half, Aristotle believed you could keep cutting it forever: you would never encounter an "uncuttable" particle.

His arguments against atomic theory focused on two primary concepts.

### The four (and five) elements

Aristotle championed the idea that all matter on Earth is made of varying combinations of four core elements, each possessing a unique pair of qualities:

| Element | Qualities |
|---|---|
| Fire | hot and dry |
| Air | hot and wet |
| Water | cold and wet |
| Earth | cold and dry |

He later added a fifth element called aether (or quintessence), an unchangeable, weightless substance that he believed made up the heavens and celestial bodies.

### The void

Democritus's atoms needed something to move in. If matter is made of separate particles, there must be empty space between them, and the atomists said so plainly: everything is atoms and the void. Aristotle attacked the void itself. He argued that truly empty space is impossible: a thing falling through nothing would meet no resistance and move infinitely fast, which is absurd, and so nothing can be empty. "Nature abhors a vacuum" is the later Latin summary of his position. Without a void, atoms have nowhere to move, and the whole atomic picture collapses. Matter must be continuous, filling every space completely.

These two arguments, the four elements and the impossible void, held for about two thousand years. They held because they matched what people saw: fire rises, stones fall, water flows, and nobody had ever observed an empty space. The alchemists worked inside this picture. If all matter is only four elements in different proportions, then changing the proportions should change lead into gold. The search for that recipe kept chemistry busy for centuries and produced its laboratory skills: distillation, calcination, the balance, the furnace, and careful notes.

## 3. Boyle: the void exists, and an element is what experiment says it is

The first pillar to fall was the void. In 1643 Torricelli filled a glass tube with mercury, turned it upside down into a dish, and the mercury dropped, leaving a space at the top with nothing in it. In 1654 von Guericke built an air pump and pumped the air out of two copper hemispheres; sixteen horses could not pull them apart. Empty space could be made on a table and its effects measured. Robert Boyle built his own improved pump in 1659 and used it for years: a bell stopped ringing inside it, a candle went out, a bird died, and the pressure of the remaining air followed a simple rule, the volume shrinking as the pressure grew. The thing Aristotle had called impossible was a standard piece of laboratory equipment.

Boyle then went after the second pillar. In The Sceptical Chymist (1661) he refused to accept fire, air, water and earth as the elements, because no experiment had ever shown that these four were what every substance is made of. He gave a different definition: an element is a substance that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by any chemical means. This is a test, not a philosophy. Whether something is an element is now decided in the laboratory, by trying to decompose it and failing. Boyle also thought of matter as made of tiny corpuscles, particles whose motions and arrangements explained chemical change. Atomos was back, two thousand years later, but with a difference: it came in as a working hypothesis that experiments could support or refute, not as an argument about cutting things in half.

## 4. Lavoisier: weigh everything

Boyle's definition made the question answerable, and Antoine Lavoisier answered it with a balance. His rule was that nothing is understood until it is weighed, before and after. Weighing before and after gave the law of conservation of mass: in a chemical reaction, the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the starting materials. Matter is not created or destroyed, only rearranged. Every equation a student balances rests on this sentence.

The balance also settled what the elements were. When Lavoisier burned substances in sealed vessels, the product weighed more than the metal, and the air inside weighed less by the same amount: burning was combination with a part of the air, which he named oxygen (1777). He showed that water, Aristotle's element, is not an element at all: it can be made from hydrogen and oxygen and decomposed back into them (1783). Air was a mixture of oxygen and nitrogen. Two of the four Greek elements were gone, taken apart on a balance.

In his Traité élémentaire de chimie (1789) Lavoisier published a table of 33 elements, each one a substance his laboratory had not been able to decompose: oxygen, nitrogen, hydrogen, sulfur, phosphorus, carbon, the known metals, and others. The list contained mistakes, among them light and "caloric", the supposed substance of heat, which were not substances at all, and lime and several other earths that were later shown to be compounds. The method survived the mistakes. From 1789 on, an element is an experimental fact, found by decomposition and weighed, and the list of elements is something that grows as the laboratory improves.

Aristotle's two arguments were now both gone. The void existed and could be pumped. The four elements had become thirty-three, found by experiment, with more to come. What remained open was the question Democritus had asked and nobody could yet answer by experiment: what are the smallest pieces, and how do they combine?
