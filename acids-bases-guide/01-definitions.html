Definitions and Classification

Three definitions

Three theories define acids and bases. Each one is more inclusive than the one before it: every Arrhenius acid is also a Brønsted-Lowry acid, and every Brønsted-Lowry acid is also a Lewis acid.

Arrhenius

An Arrhenius acid ionizes in water to produce H+ ions.

HCl(g) → H+(aq) + Cl−(aq)

An Arrhenius base dissociates in water to produce OH− ions.

NaOH(s) → Na+(aq) + OH−(aq)

The problem with the Arrhenius theory is that it does not explain some acid-base behaviors.

  • Molecular substances such as NH3 dissolve in water to form basic solutions, and some ionic compounds such as Na2CO3 or Na2O do too. Neither of these dissolve to give OH− ions directly.
  • Molecular substances such as CO2 dissolve in water to form acidic solutions, even though they do not contain H+ ions: CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3−.

Brønsted-Lowry

A Brønsted-Lowry acid is a proton donor.

HF(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ H3O+(aq) + F−(aq)

A Brønsted-Lowry base is a proton acceptor. It needs an atom with an unshared pair of electrons, because that pair is where the proton attaches.

The O–H bond electrons stay with the oxygen, which leaves as OH−.

One species, three names. Hydrogen ion H+, proton, hydronium ion H3O+: in water they are the same thing, and the real cluster is bigger still (H5O2+ and up). This guide writes H+ and leaves water out of the acid equation unless the question asks for it: HA ⇌ H+ + A−.

This definition fixes both Arrhenius problems. Ammonia is a base because it takes a proton; the OH− comes from the water. Carbon dioxide gives an acidic solution because the H2CO3 it forms gives up a proton. Nothing in the definition mentions water or OH−, so it works in any solvent, and every Arrhenius acid and base passes it too.

Lewis

A Lewis acid accepts a pair of electrons. A Lewis base donates one. A metal cation is the typical acid: it has empty orbitals and nothing else to offer.

Both electrons in each new bond came from the nitrogen. That is a coordinate covalent bond, and no proton moved to make it.

Hydroxide does the same thing to a proton: OH− + H+ → H2O. The proton is accepting the pair, so every Brønsted-Lowry reaction is also a Lewis reaction. What Lewis adds are the reactions with no proton in them at all: metal cations such as Cu2+, Al3+ and Fe3+, and molecules with an empty orbital such as BF3, are acids that Brønsted-Lowry cannot see. More on them at the end of this section.

Conjugate acid-base pairs

What is left of an acid once it has given up its proton? Its conjugate base (CB). When a base takes a proton, what forms is its conjugate acid (CA). The difference between CA and CB in a conjugate pair is one proton (H+). The pair goes by several names (conjugate acid-base pair, acid-conjugate base pair, and so on); they all mean the same two species.

Acid dissociation

An acid dissociates: it gives its proton to water. Two pairs appear, one from the acid and one from the water.

Base hydrolysis

A base hydrolyzes: it takes a proton from water.

Hydrolysis means splitting by water (hydro, water; lysis, breaking). Here, the water is what splits: the H goes to the base, and the OH− is left behind.

Water is amphoteric

Water is on both diagrams, once as the base and once as the acid. A substance that can do either is amphoteric, and water does it even with itself.

with an acid, water takes the proton:H2O(l) + HCl(aq) ⇌ H3O+(aq) + Cl−(aq) with a base, water gives a proton:H2O(l) + NH3(aq) ⇌ NH4+(aq) + OH−(aq) pure water autoionizes; one molecule gives a proton, another takes it:H2O(l) + H2O(l) ⇌ H3O+(aq) + OH−(aq)

Structure of acids

Which hydrogen in an acid comes off? Acids are sorted by where that hydrogen sits.

TypeExamplesWhere the acidic hydrogen sits
Binary acidsHCl, HF, HBr, H2SOn a nonmetal atom. Inorganic.
OxyacidsH2SO4, HNO3, HClO, H3PO4On an oxygen atom. Inorganic.
Carboxylic acidsHC2H3O2 (acetic acid, also written CH3COOH), HCOOH (formic acid), H3C6H5O7 (citric acid)On the oxygen of a COOH group. Only that H ionizes; the hydrogens on carbon never do. Organic.

In HC2H3O2 the acidic hydrogen is written first, apart from the three on carbon. CH3COOH is the same acid written in the order the atoms are bonded, and there the acidic hydrogen is the last one.

How many protons can an acid give? A monoprotic acid gives one (HCl, HNO3, CH3COOH), a diprotic acid two (H2SO4, H2CO3), a triprotic acid three (H3PO4). Two or more makes it polyprotic. Count only the hydrogens that ionize: acetic acid has four H and gives one, so it is monoprotic.

Structure of bases

Does a base need OH− in its formula? No. Bases come in two kinds, and only one of them contains OH−.

TypeExamplesWhere the OH− comes from
HydroxidesNaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)2, Ba(OH)2From the base itself: ionic, releases OH− as is. Strong when the metal is Group 1A or heavy Group 2A.
AminesNH3, CH3NH2 (methylamine), (CH3)2NH, C5H5N (pyridine)From the water: the lone pair on N takes a proton from H2O. All weak.
Anions of weak acidsCO32−, F−, CH3COO−From the water: the anion takes a proton from H2O. This is why Na2CO3 gives a basic solution.
CH3NH2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ CH3NH3+(aq) + OH−(aq)

So ammonia is a base with no OH− in it. Its nitrogen holds a lone pair, the lone pair takes a proton from water, and the OH− left behind is what makes the solution basic.

How many protons can a base take? A monoacidic base takes one, or releases one OH− (NaOH, NH3). A polyacidic base takes two or more (Ca(OH)2 releases two OH−; hydrazine, N2H4, has a lone pair on each nitrogen and takes two protons).

Strong and weak

Does every molecule of an acid give up its proton? A strong acid dissociates completely: essentially every molecule gives up its proton, and the reaction is written with a one-way arrow. A weak acid dissociates partly: some molecules are dissociated, most are not, and the reaction is written as an equilibrium.

strongHCl(aq) → H+(aq) + Cl−(aq) weakHNO2(aq) ⇌ H+(aq) + NO2−(aq)

The same words apply to bases. A strong base dissociates completely into its ions (NaOH); a weak base takes a proton from water only partly (NH3). Strength indicates the degree of dissociation, the fraction dissociated. Do not confuse strength with concentration: dilute hydrochloric acid is 10−6 M and concentrated hydrochloric acid is 12 M, and both are strong. Seven acids and eight bases are strong; everything else is weak. Section 2 lists them and shows how the two kinds behave.

Practice

  1. For each substance, state under which theory or theories it counts as an acid, and under which as a base. Some fit more than one.
    1. HBr
    2. KOH
    3. NH3
    4. BF3
    5. CH3NH2
    6. Fe3+
  2. Write the conjugate base of each acid: HNO2, H2PO4−, NH4+, H2O, HSO4−.
  3. Write the conjugate acid of each base: F−, HCO3−, CH3NH2, OH−, H2O.
  4. For the reaction HCO3−(aq) + HF(aq) ⇌ H2CO3(aq) + F−(aq), label the acid, the base, the conjugate acid and the conjugate base, and write the two conjugate pairs.
  5. Classify each acid as binary, oxyacid or carboxylic, say whether it is organic or inorganic, and point to the acidic hydrogen: HI, HClO, CH3COOH, HNO3, HCOOH, H2S.
  6. Classify each base as a hydroxide or an amine, and say which ones release OH− directly: Ba(OH)2, (CH3)2NH, C5H5N, LiOH.
  7. Identify the Lewis acid and the Lewis base in each reaction, and say whether a proton is transferred.
    1. NH3 + H+ → NH4+
    2. Ag+ + 2 NH3 → [Ag(NH3)2]+
    3. (CH3)2O + BF3 → (CH3)2O–BF3
    4. Cl− + AlCl3 → AlCl4−

Answers

    1. HBr: acid under all three. Arrhenius (produces H+ in water), Brønsted-Lowry (donates a proton), Lewis (the proton it donates accepts an electron pair).
    2. KOH: base under all three. Arrhenius (produces OH−), Brønsted-Lowry and Lewis (the OH− accepts a proton and donates an electron pair).
    3. NH3: base under Brønsted-Lowry (proton acceptor) and Lewis (lone pair donor). Not an Arrhenius base: no OH− in the formula.
    4. BF3: Lewis acid only. It has no proton to donate and an empty orbital on boron.
    5. CH3NH2: base under Brønsted-Lowry and Lewis, like ammonia. Not Arrhenius.
    6. Fe3+: Lewis acid (accepts electron pairs from water). Its hydrated ion Fe(H2O)63+ is also a Brønsted-Lowry acid: it donates a proton to give Fe(H2O)5(OH)2+.
  2. Remove one H+ (charge drops by one): NO2−, HPO42−, NH3, OH−, SO42−.
  3. Add one H+ (charge rises by one): HF, H2CO3, CH3NH3+, H2O, H3O+.
  4. HF is the acid (loses a proton), HCO3− is the base (gains a proton). H2CO3 is the conjugate acid, F− is the conjugate base. Pairs: HF / F− and H2CO3 / HCO3−.
    • HI: binary, inorganic; the H on iodine.
    • HClO: oxyacid, inorganic; the H on oxygen (H–O–Cl).
    • CH3COOH: carboxylic, organic; the H on the COOH oxygen. The three H on carbon never ionize.
    • HNO3: oxyacid, inorganic; the H on oxygen.
    • HCOOH: carboxylic, organic; the H on the COOH oxygen, not the H on carbon.
    • H2S: binary, inorganic; the H on sulfur.
  6. Ba(OH)2 and LiOH are hydroxides and release OH− directly on dissolving. (CH3)2NH (dimethylamine) and C5H5N (pyridine) are amines: the OH− in their solutions comes from water after the nitrogen takes a proton.
    1. Lewis acid H+, Lewis base NH3. A proton is transferred, so it is also a Brønsted-Lowry reaction.
    2. Lewis acid Ag+, Lewis base NH3. No proton transfer; the pH does not change.
    3. Lewis acid BF3, Lewis base (CH3)2O (lone pair on oxygen). No proton transfer.
    4. Lewis acid AlCl3 (empty orbital on aluminum), Lewis base Cl−. No proton transfer.
